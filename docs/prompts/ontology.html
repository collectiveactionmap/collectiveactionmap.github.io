Collective-action mechanism ontology

Approved W07 mechanism ontology (v4): 30 mechanisms organised by collective action type, high-level mechanism and fine-level mechanism. Definitions are reproduced from the supplied ontology CSV.

30 mechanisms
Mechanism IDCollective action typeHigh-level mechanismFine-level mechanismDefinition
M1.1.1Collective market shapingDeliver incentives for improved performancePreferential purchase agreements Coordinated purchasing arrangements in which buyers offer producers, suppliers or sourcing landscapes preferential terms, such as longer-term contracts, assured demand, minimum pricing and/or price premiums, which are conditional on agreed environmental and/or resilience-related performance. The coordination may be among buyers (several buyers offering common terms) or with the supply side (terms agreed with a producer group, cooperative or a whole sourcing landscape). The defining feature is that the preferential terms are attached to buying the product and depend on performance. Typical forms include long-term offtake or sourcing agreements, landscape or jurisdictional sourcing commitments, guaranteed purchase volumes, floor prices and sustainability premiums. Preferential terms offered by one company acting alone to its own suppliers are not this mechanism. Payments for environmental services or performance that are not attached to buying the product are M1.1.2 (payment for environmental services). A buyer alliance’s wider coordination of requirements and signals is M1.1.3 (buyer alliances), and both can be coded when a text describes both.
M1.1.2Collective market shapingDeliver incentives for improved performancePayment for environmental services or improved performancePayments or other financial rewards made to any actor in return for delivering or demonstrating specified environmental services or improvements in sustainability performance, thereby creating a direct commercial incentive for change. Here the payment is made by, or channelled through, supply-chain actors such as buyers, traders, brands or processors, as part of a market-shaping initiative. Recipients may be producers, suppliers, communities or other actors who maintain the service. The payment rewards the service or performance itself rather than the purchase of a product. Typical forms include performance-based or results-based payments, conservation or deforestation-free bonuses, and payments to communities for forest protection funded by supply-chain actors. Premiums or other terms built into a purchase agreement are M1.1.1 (preferential purchase agreements). Payments channelled through a financing arrangement or an environmental credit market (carbon or biodiversity credits, public or donor-funded schemes) are M5.1.3 (payment for environmental services, finance).
M1.1.3Collective market shapingDeliver incentives for improved performanceBuyer AlliancesA group of purchasing organisations that coordinates sourcing requirements, purchasing commitments or market signals to encourage suppliers or production regions to adopt shared sustainability practices. Members are buyers of the commodity, such as manufacturers, retailers, traders or processors, who act together rather than individually. This is typically through a named coalition, platform, working group or pre-competitive collaboration. Typical activities include agreeing common sourcing policies or supplier requirements, engaging suppliers jointly, signalling future demand together and pooling leverage over suppliers or regions. Code this mechanism for the alliance and its coordinating role. Code the specific instruments it uses separately as well, for example M1.1.1 (preferential purchase agreements), M2.1.1 (collective market exclusion) or M3.2.1 (collective commitments).
M2.1.1Market access restrictionsBlocking or limiting purchases of volumes or products that do not meet sustainability criteriaCollective market exclusionAn agreement among multiple MARKET actors to suspend, refuse or restrict purchases of specified products, origins or suppliers unless they meet collectively agreed sustainability conditions. The actors are private market participants, such as traders, processors, manufacturers or retailers, acting without a legal mandate. The defining feature is a shared rule that non-compliant products, origins or suppliers are excluded from purchase. Typical forms include sector-wide moratoria, collective no-buy or cut-off lists, joint supplier suspension protocols and industry agreements not to source from specified areas after a cut-off date. Incentives that reward compliance rather than exclude non-compliance are M1.1.1 or M1.1.2. Restrictions imposed by law are M2.1.2 (government legislation). If governments, NGOs or other non-market actors jointly design, implement or enforce the restriction, code M2.1.3 (multi-stakeholder market exclusion).
M2.1.2Market access restrictionsBlocking or limiting purchases of volumes or products that do not meet sustainability criteriaDemand side government market restrictions legislationGovernment-imposed legal requirements in importing or consumer markets, whether international or domestic, that restrict the placing on the market, import or sale of commodities or products that fail specified environmental or sustainability conditions. The defining feature is a binding legal rule set by a public authority that controls what may be imported, placed on the market or sold, rather than regulating how commodities are produced. Typical forms include import bans, prohibitions on placing illegally produced or deforestation-linked products on the market, and mandatory due-diligence or traceability obligations that operators must meet before products can be placed on the market. Voluntary government guidance is not this mechanism, and nor are trade-agreement provisions that do not restrict market access. Producer-country regulation of production is M4.1.1 (support producer country policies and systems). Where the legislation is combined with collective buyer or supply-chain commitments in one arrangement, also code M2.1.3.
M2.1.3Market access restrictionsBlocking or limiting purchases of volumes or products that do not meet sustainability criteriaMulti-stakeholder market exclusionA market-access restriction jointly designed, implemented or enforced through multiple stakeholders with different roles in supply chains and/or production systems, for example combining regulatory measures with collective buyer or supply-chain commitments. The stakeholders include at least two different types of actor, for example government agencies, public prosecutors, companies, NGOs, producer organisations or financial institutions. The arrangement still works by excluding or restricting purchases of non-compliant products, origins or suppliers. Typical forms include agreements in which public authorities and companies jointly enforce a sourcing restriction, moratoria monitored or verified by NGOs or government agencies, and legally binding agreements that companies sign with public authorities. Restrictions agreed among market actors alone are M2.1.1; restrictions imposed by legislation alone are M2.1.2.
M3.1.1ComplianceCommon understanding and management of riskSector- and/or area-level risk assessment to identify priority high-risk regions A coordinated process for identifying priority high-risk regions with regards to deforestation, conversion, biodiversity loss, greenhouse gas emissions across a sector or geographic area, using shared data and/or methods to inform supply-chain compliance and risk management. The defining output is a shared prioritisation: an agreed list or map of the regions, municipalities, districts or sourcing areas where risk is highest. Participating companies then use it to focus due diligence, supplier engagement or investment. It can exist without any shared ongoing monitoring or reporting. Typical forms include sector-wide identification of priority or high-risk municipalities, jointly commissioned risk assessments and shared risk classifications of origins. Ongoing mapping, monitoring and reporting of risk over time is M3.1.2. A risk assessment carried out within a single landscape to guide that landscape’s own land-use decisions is M6.1.2 (landscape and area level risk mapping).
M3.1.2ComplianceCommon understanding and management of riskCoordinated mapping, monitoring and reportingA coordinated process for mapping, monitoring, reporting and/or communicating environmental or sustainability risks across a sector or geographic area, using shared data and/or methods to inform supply-chain compliance and risk management. This can include, but is not restricted to, satellite- and community-based mapping. The defining feature is shared, ongoing tracking of risk or change over time. Typical forms include jointly funded deforestation or land-use-change alerts, shared satellite monitoring platforms, supplier or mill mapping pooled across companies, community or participatory monitoring, and agreed formats for reporting detected risks to participants. A one-off exercise to identify priority high-risk regions is M3.1.1. Jointly checking whether specific supplier or production information is accurate is M4.1.3 (shared monitoring and verification). Reporting progress against a landscape’s agreed objectives is M6.2.2 (landscape level progress reporting).
M3.1.3ComplianceCommon understanding and management of riskSupplier and/or area level grievance mechanismA shared process through which affected stakeholders can raise concerns about suppliers or activities in a sourcing area, have complaints assessed, and seek responses or remedy across participating organisations. The same system can also be used by sourcing companies/suppliers to address the grievance. The process is shared by several companies or organisations rather than run by one company for its own operations. It covers receiving, assessing and responding to complaints. Typical forms include industry-wide or multi-company grievance platforms, jointly managed grievance logs and trackers, shared procedures for handling or verifying cases, and collective engagement with a supplier named in a grievance, including support to affected stakeholders. Grievances may concern environmental harms, such as deforestation or conversion, or social harms, such as land rights or labour.
M3.1.4ComplianceCommon understanding and management of riskArea based and/or sector level traceability for compliance specificallyA shared system that records and links information about the origin, movement or handling of commodities across suppliers or geographic areas, primarily to demonstrate compliance with sourcing, due-diligence or market-access requirements. The system is shared by several companies, or across a sector or area, rather than built by one company for its own supply chain. It may span the chain from production to the point where products enter the market. Its defining feature is its purpose: producing traceability evidence that meets compliance requirements, such as deforestation-free commitments, due-diligence legislation or certification. Typical forms include shared traceability platforms and data standards, and supply-base mapping built jointly for due-diligence statements. Traceability infrastructure built or strengthened within a producing country or sourcing area for wider supply-chain management is M4.1.2. Where a system serves both purposes, code both.
M3.2.1ComplianceAligned indicators and approachesCollective commitments Publicly stated and formally agreed OR informally agreed collective commitment to achieve shared objectives through which participating actors align actions and track progress on environmental or sustainability outcomes. The defining feature is that several actors commit together to the same objective or target. Examples are a sector pledge to end deforestation by a given date, a joint declaration, or a manifesto or statement of support signed by multiple organisations. Individual company commitments that were not made jointly are not this mechanism, even when many companies have made similar ones. Where the commitment also sets out shared milestones and implementation pathways, code M3.2.2 (collective road maps) separately as well.
M3.2.2ComplianceAligned indicators and approachesCollective road mapsPublicly stated and formally agreed OR informally agreed collective commitment to shared milestones and implementation pathways through which participating actors align actions and track progress on environmental or sustainability outcomes. The defining feature is a shared plan for how and when an objective will be reached: time-bound milestones, interim targets, sequenced actions or responsibilities agreed by the participants. Typical forms include sector or commodity roadmaps, joint action plans, frameworks for action, and implementation plans attached to a collective commitment. A commitment to an objective alone, without shared milestones or implementation pathways, is M3.2.1 only.
M3.2.3ComplianceAligned indicators and approachesSector or area level databases and dashboardsShared digital repositories, registries or dashboards that aggregate and make accessible comparable sustainability, sourcing, compliance or performance information across participating organisations, sectors or areas. The defining feature is the shared information tool itself: a platform that holds data from or about several organisations, sectors or areas and makes it available to participants or the public. Typical forms include supply-chain transparency platforms, sector-wide supplier or mill registries, company-performance trackers and public progress dashboards. The rules for what is reported are M3.2.4 (common reporting frameworks). Generating new monitoring data is M3.1.2 or M4.1.3. A dashboard of a landscape’s progress against its own agreed objectives is M6.2.2. Where the text describes both the tool and the process that feeds it, code both separately.
M3.2.4ComplianceAligned indicators and approachesCommon reporting frameworksCommon definitions, metrics, reporting rules and disclosure formats agreed or adopted by multiple organisations to enable consistent reporting and comparison of environmental or sustainability performance. The defining feature is a shared rulebook for what is reported and how. Examples are common definitions of deforestation-free or conversion-free, agreed cut-off dates, shared indicator sets, disclosure templates and reporting guidance used across companies or initiatives. It counts whether the participants developed the framework themselves or adopted it from an external initiative. The platform where reported data are held is M3.2.3. A standard with assessment of conformity is M3.2.5 (certification).
M3.2.5ComplianceAligned indicators and approachesCertification A collective standards-and-assurance system that establishes requirements and verifies conformity for a commodity, product, producer, volume, area or jurisdiction, potentially allowing certified claims or market recognition. The defining feature is a written standard combined with a process for checking conformity against it. This covers voluntary sustainability standards and certification schemes: product, chain-of-custody, mass-balance or book-and-claim, group, and jurisdictional or landscape certification. Schemes verified by an independent third party and schemes verified by other means (second-party or self-assessment) both count. Commitments or reporting rules without an assessment of conformity are M3.2.1 or M3.2.4. Verification of supply-chain information outside a certification standard is M4.1.3.
M4.1.1Improving operational systemsImprove producer country practices and systemsSupport producer country policies and systemsCoordinated technical, financial or institutional assistance to strengthen policies, regulations, enforcement or administrative systems in producing countries so that sustainable sourcing and production can be implemented effectively. The assistance is provided jointly by several actors, such as companies, donors, NGOs or international organisations, to producer-country governments or public agencies, or is designed together with them. Typical forms include support for land registration and tenure systems, enforcement of forest or land-use law, licensing and permit systems, and national or subnational commodity strategies. Training aimed directly at producers or other practitioners is M4.2.1 (capacity building). Support for national traceability systems is also M4.1.2. Laws restricting market access in consumer markets are M2.1.2. Where the support is part of a landscape or jurisdictional programme, also code M4.1.4.
M4.1.2Improving operational systemsImprove producer country practices and systemsArea based and/or sector level traceability for any reasonJoint development or strengthening of operational traceability infrastructure, data-sharing arrangements or procedures for one or more sectors within a producing country or sourcing area to track commodities back to their origins and support improved supply-chain management. The focus is the first mile: linking farms, plots, producer groups or forest areas to the first points of collection, aggregation or processing. The purpose can be anything, including compliance, productivity, quality, premiums or producer support. Typical forms include national or sector-wide traceability systems, registration or mapping of farmers and plots, producer identification cards, supplier databases shared among companies sourcing from the same area, and common data-sharing protocols. Traceability systems built across wider supply chains specifically to demonstrate compliance are M3.1.4. Where a system does both, code both separately.
M4.1.3Improving operational systemsImprove producer country practices and systemsShared monitoring and verificationA coordinated arrangement in which participating actors jointly collect, check or independently verify environmental, production or supply-chain information using shared protocols or monitoring infrastructure. The defining feature is checking whether information is accurate, for example whether suppliers, farms or areas meet stated requirements. The checking uses shared protocols, shared field or remote-sensing infrastructure, or a jointly appointed verifier. Typical forms include joint supplier audits or assessments, shared verification of deforestation-free or legality claims, jointly funded field checks of satellite alerts, and independent monitoring bodies serving several organisations. Mapping and monitoring that tracks risk across a sector or area without verifying specific claims is M3.1.2. Verification within a certification scheme is M3.2.5.
M4.1.4Improving operational systemsImprove producer country practices and systemsLandscape and jurisdictional approaches to improving producer country practices and systemsA coordinated programme operating across a defined production area, landscape or jurisdiction that aligns producers, companies, local stakeholders and public authorities to improve production practices, institutions or supply-chain systems beyond individual farms or firms. Not every group listed needs to be involved, but more than one type of actor must be. The defining features are a geographical boundary (a landscape, watershed, district, municipality, province or state) and a focus on changing how production and its supporting systems work across that area. Typical activities include area-wide programmes to raise productivity or spread sustainable practices, strengthening producer organisations and local institutions, and improving local supply chains together. These programmes often use other mechanisms too, which should be coded separately (for example M4.2.1, M4.1.2 or M6.2.1). A landscape programme mainly about planning where conservation, restoration and production happen is M6.1.1. Code both when the text describes both.
M4.2.1Improving operational systemsShare good practices and lessons learnedCapacity buildingJoint provision or exchange of practical knowledge, technical assistance and training materials or activities to strengthen the ability of producers or other actors to adopt and maintain improved practices. This can include exchange of manuals, toolkits, Train the Trainer (TTT) guidance, Farmer Field School learning process, etc. Joint means the knowledge, assistance or training is developed, funded, delivered or shared by several organisations together, or offered across a sector or area, rather than by one company training its own suppliers. Recipients can be producers, producer organisations, suppliers, companies, extension workers or local institutions. Typical forms also include shared extension programmes, demonstration plots, peer-to-peer learning networks, communities of practice and pooled good-practice guidance. Institutional support to producer-country governments’ policies and systems is M4.1.1.
M5.1.1Supply chain financing and insuranceIntegrate nature into finance decisionsGreen / Sustainability bondsDebt instruments whose proceeds or financial terms are earmarked for, or linked to, specified environmental or sustainability investments or outcomes, used to mobilise collective finance through coordinated supply-chain or landscape initiatives, therefore leveraging multiple investment flows via one instrument. Typical forms include green, sustainability and sustainability-linked bonds and similar labelled debt, issued by or for a coordinated initiative, a group of companies, a landscape programme or a financing facility serving several actors. A bond issued by a single company for its own operations, outside any coordinated initiative, is not this mechanism. Where the bond is structured with public, philanthropic or concessional capital alongside commercial capital, also code M5.1.2 (blended finance). Where some investors take a first-loss position, also code M5.2.1 (first loss pools).
M5.1.2Supply chain financing and insuranceIntegrate nature into finance decisionsBlended financeA financing structure combining public, philanthropic and/or concessional capital with commercial investment to reduce barriers, share costs or risks, and mobilise funding for environmental or sustainable production activities. The defining feature is that public, philanthropic or concessional money is used deliberately to make commercial investment possible or attractive. Instruments include guarantees, subordinated or concessional loans, grant-funded technical assistance, and tiered funds and facilities. Funding from only one kind of source (only public, only philanthropic or only commercial) is not this mechanism. Where the structure includes a defined first-loss tranche, also code M5.2.1. Where it issues labelled bonds, also code M5.1.1.
M5.1.3Supply chain financing and insuranceIntegrate nature into finance decisionsPayment for environmental services (incl. carbon credits, biodiversity credits)A financing arrangement that channels payments to land managers or other actors for verified environmental services or outcomes, potentially funded through carbon, biodiversity or other environmental credit markets. There are two defining features. Payment depends on a verified environmental service or outcome, such as avoided deforestation, carbon storage, restoration, biodiversity or water services. And the payment flows through a financing arrangement, not as part of a commercial purchase of a commodity. Typical forms include carbon and biodiversity credit projects, jurisdictional REDD+ and other results-based payment programmes, and payment-for-ecosystem-services schemes run by governments, donors or funds. Payments or rewards made by or through supply-chain actors as part of their commercial relationships are M1.1.2.
M5.2.1Supply chain financing and insuranceRisk sharing instrumentsFirst loss poolsA pooled financing arrangement in which designated capital providers agree to absorb an initial portion of investment losses, reducing downside exposure for other investors and enabling financing of higher-risk sustainability activities. The defining feature is a layered capital structure in which one tier, often public, philanthropic or development finance, takes losses first so that the other tiers are protected. Typical terms include first-loss capital or tranche, junior or subordinated equity or debt, catalytic capital, and guarantees covering initial losses. A first-loss tranche or guarantee inside a blended finance structure counts here; also code M5.1.2.
M5.2.2Supply chain financing and insuranceRisk sharing instrumentsPooled risk insuranceAn insurance mechanism in which several participants combine premiums, exposure or reserves to share specified financial losses arising from environmental, production or supply-chain risks. Typical forms include risk pools, mutual or cooperative insurance, group or area-based weather or crop index insurance, parametric insurance covering a sourcing area or producer group, and shared reserve or contingency funds. Risks covered can include drought, flood, fire, pests, disease and yield loss. A conventional insurance policy bought by one company or one producer for its own risk is not this mechanism.
M6.1.1Area level sourcing and place based improvementShared land use planning, including area conservation and restorationLandscape and jurisdictional approaches to shared land use planningA coordinated, place-based programme bringing stakeholders across a landscape or jurisdiction together to plan and implement conservation, restoration and commodity production land use across public, private and community owned landholdings. The defining feature is a multi-stakeholder process that decides where and how land across a defined landscape or jurisdiction will be conserved, restored or used for production, and then acts on those decisions. Typical forms include jurisdictional or landscape initiatives built around a shared land-use vision, agreements or compacts balancing production and protection, and programmes that map and pursue restoration and conservation opportunities. The spatial plan or zoning itself is M6.1.3. The governance arrangement running the programme is M6.2.1. A landscape programme focused on improving production practices and systems rather than land allocation is M4.1.4. Code each one the text describes.
M6.1.2Area level sourcing and place based improvementShared land use planning, including area conservation and restorationLandscape and area level risk mappingA collaborative spatial assessment that identifies and prioritises environmental risks, sensitive areas, risk of biodiversity loss, risks of forest loss, risk of conversion or intervention needs within a landscape, to guide collective land-use decisions and investments. The focus is risk of loss or degradation linked to commodity production within one landscape. The landscape’s stakeholders assess it together and use it as an input to the landscape’s planning and investment. Typical forms include participatory or multi-stakeholder mapping of forests, high conservation value or high carbon stock areas, and areas at risk of conversion. Mapping restoration opportunities belongs to land-use planning (M6.1.1 or M6.1.3). Identifying priority high-risk regions across a sector or wider region for supply-chain compliance is M3.1.1.
M6.1.3Area level sourcing and place based improvementShared land use planning, including area conservation and restorationArea level land-use management planningA jointly developed or coordinated spatial plan that sets agreed land-use objectives, zones, management rules or priority actions for production, conservation or restoration across a defined area. The defining feature is the plan itself, as a document or an agreed set of zones and rules, rather than the programme around it. Typical forms include participatory or jurisdictional land-use plans, zoning plans, integrated landscape management plans, and village, district or watershed plans with production, conservation and restoration zones. A plan made by a government agency alone counts only if it was developed with, or is implemented together with, other stakeholders. The process and partnership that produce and implement the plan are M6.1.1 and M6.2.1.
M6.2.1Area level sourcing and place based improvementAccountability and reportingMulti-partnership governance of place-based initiativesA formal or informal governance arrangement for a place-based, landscape-based or area-based initiative through which multiple organisations or stakeholder groups share decision-making, oversight, responsibilities or accountability. The defining features are how the initiative is governed - who takes part, how decisions are made and who holds whom to account- and that the initiative operates at the level of an area, place or landscape. Typical forms include multi-stakeholder platforms, steering committees or boards, landscape or jurisdictional compacts and secretariats, and agreements dividing roles among government, companies, producers, communities and NGOs. Code this mechanism when the text describes the governance arrangement, alongside whichever mechanism describes what the initiative does (for example M4.1.4 or M6.1.1). Governance of a sector-wide initiative that is not tied to a place is not this mechanism.
M6.2.2Area level sourcing and place based improvementAccountability and reportingLandscape level progress reportingA shared process for compiling, assessing continuous improvement and communicating progress against agreed environmental, land-use or livelihood objectives at the landscape or jurisdictional scale, using indicators or evidence from multiple actors. The defining features are landscape or jurisdictional scale, objectives agreed by the landscape’s stakeholders, and reporting repeated over time so that change can be tracked. Typical forms include landscape assessment and verification frameworks, jurisdictional performance reports, landscape scorecards or dashboards, and periodic public progress reports. Company-level or sector-wide reporting is M3.2.4 or M3.2.3. Ongoing monitoring of deforestation or risk that is not reported against agreed landscape objectives is M3.1.2.
